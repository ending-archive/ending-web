The glasshouse stood at the end of the garden like a lantern someone had forgotten to light. Petra's grandmother had grown tomatoes in it, and orchids, and for one disastrous summer a lemon tree that sulked.

Petra inherited the key with the house. For a long time she did not use it. The glass fogged, the paint blistered, and a single determined vine pushed through a broken pane and began to claim the interior.

In the third year she unlocked the door. The air inside was thick and green and smelled of earth that remembered being loved. She stood in the doorway and understood that the glasshouse had never been neglected, only waiting, as patient things do.

She started with the vine. She did not cut it back; she built it a trellis. Then she cleared one bench, then two, and sowed the first tray of seeds with hands that remembered the movement before she did.

By midsummer the glass was clear again, and the light that came through it fell across the benches in long gold stripes. Petra sat on an upturned crate with a mug of tea going cold and watched the first green shoots lean toward the sun.

Her grandmother had always said a glasshouse was just a way of persuading the sky to stay a while. Petra thought now that it also worked on people.
