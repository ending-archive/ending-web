Chapter One

By the winter of 1974 the Skerry Light was the last on the coast still tended by a human hand, and Tobiah Marsh was the hand. The Board wrote every autumn to say it would be automated by spring. Every spring it was not.

He climbed the ninety-one steps each dusk, lit the lamp, and spoke to the sea in the offhand way one speaks to a difficult relative.

Chapter Two

The storm arrived on the third of December without the courtesy of a warning. By midnight the spray was reaching the lantern room, forty metres above the rocks, and the whole tower hummed like a struck bell.

Tobiah did not sleep. He kept the lamp turning and the logbook current, entry after steady entry, because a record of calm in the midst of chaos was, he believed, a kind of lighthouse of its own.

Chapter Three

At dawn the sea fell quiet, and a small red boat sat wedged in the rocks below with three frightened fishermen aboard, alive because they had steered by a light that was not supposed to still exist.

The Board's letter came in March. This time Tobiah did not read it. He folded it into the logbook and went up to trim the wick.
